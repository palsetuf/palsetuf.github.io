Research

Much of my work comes back to a simple phenomenon of epistasis: in biology, parts rarely act alone. The effect of a mutation depends on the other mutations in the same protein, the role of a gene depends on the genome around it, and the success of a bacterium depends on the neighbors it meets. I build models that predict these outcomes while also revealing the interactions behind them, and the examples below follow this idea from the smallest scale to the largest.

Higher-order epistasis in proteins

Mutations in a protein often change each other's effects, and these interactions can span many positions at once. Measuring them beyond pairs has been difficult, because the number of possible combinations grows so quickly.

The epistatic transformer makes this tractable. With the softmax, layer normalization and feed-forward blocks removed, the depth of the model sets exactly how complex its interactions can be, so comparing models of different depths shows how much each level of interaction contributes. Across ten large protein datasets, higher-order epistasis turned out to matter most when predicting sequences far from the training data.

A foundation model for bacterial genomes

Most sequenced bacteria carry genes whose functions are still unknown, and protein language models usually look at each protein on its own. BacPT instead reads the whole proteome of a genome. Trained on tens of thousands of complete bacterial genomes, it learns a representation for each gene in its genomic context, along with one for the genome as a whole.

These representations improve predictions across scales, from enzyme activity and biosynthetic gene clusters to metabolic traits and interactions between species.

Background

I came to computational biology through engineering. At BITS Pilani I studied electronics and instrumentation (B.E.) alongside biological sciences (M.Sc), and my master's thesis with Sandhya Marathe predicted CRISPR-regulated self-target genes in Salmonella Typhimurium. Along the way, I worked on genome compression and DNA- and RNA-binding protein prediction with Shandar Ahmad at JNU New Delhi, and built a computer vision-based vehicle traffic estimation pipeline at the Indian Institute of Science that was later adopted for traffic control in Bangalore.

After graduating, I spent three years as a data scientist on the machine learning research team at Reliance Jio. There, I built a co-occurrence based GWAS system, modeled crude-oil refineries with neural network digital twins, and developed a sampling method for black-box systems that led to a granted US patent. In 2022, I joined the University of Florida for my PhD with Juannan Zhou, spent the summer of 2023 at Evozyne in Chicago working on protein sequence–function modeling, and was named a UF Biodiversity Institute Fellow for 2024–25.
